History of Cannondale

1971 — Not a Bike Company

Cannondale was founded in Wilton, Connecticut by Joe Montgomery and Murdock MacGregor. The name came from the nearby Cannondale train station. Their first products had nothing to do with bicycles — the company initially manufactured precast concrete housing before pivoting to outdoor equipment. Their first cycling product was the "Bugger," a bicycle-towed trailer, followed by lightweight touring bags. Within months, Cannondale became the world's largest manufacturer of lightweight bicycle bags, securing orders from over 2,500 dealers.

1983 — First Bicycle

Cannondale entered the bicycle market with the ST-500, a touring bike that made a bold statement: aluminum instead of steel. At a time when high-end cycling was dominated by European steel frames from Bianchi, Peugeot, and Colnago, Cannondale bet everything on large-diameter aluminum tubing and TIG welding. Dealers and consumers were skeptical — the oversized tubes looked strange. But the frames were lighter, stiffer, and more durable than anything else on the market.

1990s — Handmade in USA & Racing

Cannondale opened its Bedford, Pennsylvania factory, producing frames proudly labeled "Handmade in the USA." The company jumped into mountain biking, designing off-road models that quickly gained a following. The CAAD (Cannondale Advanced Aluminum Design) platform was born, setting the standard for aluminum frame performance that would dominate for over a decade. Cannondale signed the iconic Saeco road team deal for a million dollars, putting their bikes on the world's biggest stage.

2000s — Carbon, Bankruptcy, Revival

Cannondale embraced carbon fiber — on its own terms — developing frames that combined carbon with their signature aluminum expertise. But the decade brought turmoil. Founder Joe Montgomery bet big on motocross motorcycles, attempting to compete with Honda on a fraction of the budget. The venture burned through $100 million and drove Cannondale into bankruptcy in 2003. Joe lost the company he had spent 32 years building. The company was later acquired by Dorel Sports and rebuilt. In 2013, Cannondale returned to its roots, opening a new global headquarters at 1 Cannondale Way in Wilton, Connecticut.

Today

Now a subsidiary of Pon Holdings, Cannondale continues to operate as one of the most innovative brands in cycling. From the SuperSix Evo road bike to the Scalpel and F-Si mountain bikes, the company's design philosophy remains unchanged: ditch the rulebook, challenge assumptions, and build products that work better — even if they look strange doing it.

Lefty Technology — How It Works

Conventional telescoping forks use two legs: the left contains the spring, the right contains the damper. Two bushings in each leg manage sliding friction. Under braking and cornering, these bushings bind, creating stiction that ruins small-bump sensitivity. The Lefty solves this by putting everything in one leg — spring and damper combined — and replacing the second leg's bushing with needle roller bearings.

The Needle Bearing System

The Lefty's inner stanchion is not round — it's a square or triangular profile that rides on sets of needle roller bearings rather than sliding bushings. Where a traditional fork relies on two legs each with two bushings (four friction surfaces total), the Lefty uses one bushing and a single set of needle bearings that roll rather than slide. The result is dramatically lower friction — the Lefty Ocho claims 141% less static friction and 75% less dynamic friction than a Fox 32 or RockShox SID.

Stiffness Without Weight

By eliminating the second leg, the Lefty saves significant weight. But the single-leg design also creates an inherent stiffness advantage: the stanchion tube can be much larger in diameter where it meets the crown, resisting torsional flex better than two smaller tubes. The needle bearing system prevents the wheel from twisting out of alignment under load — the square-on-square (or triangle-on-triangle) interface mechanically prevents rotation. Cannondale claims they have never had a Lefty fork leg break since the design launched in 2000.

Inverted Design

The Lefty is an inverted (upside-down) fork: the larger-diameter outer tube is at the top, clamped in the crown, and the thinner stanchion extends downward to the wheel. This puts the thickest, stiffest section of the fork at the head tube where leverage forces are highest, and reduces unsprung mass at the wheel for better traction.

The History of Lefty

1992 — HeadShok

The Lefty's lineage begins with the HeadShok, Cannondale's first suspension system. Unlike conventional forks, the HeadShok housed the suspension inside the head tube, using needle bearing strips sandwiched between inner and outer races. The system was exceptionally stiff and low-friction compared to the rudimentary telescoping forks of the era. But it had a hard limit: maximum travel topped out at 80mm because the head tube could only hold so much.

1996–1998 — Moto DH & Moto FR

As travel demands grew, Cannondale moved the suspension outside the head tube. The Moto 120 fork (1996) appeared on the Super V DH4000, the first production downhill bike. It was a conventional two-leg fork but used Cannondale's needle bearing technology internally. The Moto FR (Freeride) followed in 1998 — essentially two HeadShok units, one per leg, offering 100mm of travel. It was extraordinarily stiff and bombproof, but heavy. These forks were so durable they remain sought-after today for tandem bikes.

1999 — The Accidental Invention

Legend has it that Cannondale mechanics were messing around with a Moto FR fork, trying to prove how stiff it was. They cut one leg off, bolted a hub to the remaining side, and rode it around the parking lot. It worked. The single-sided concept was viable. Cannondale engineers had already experimented with a single-crown, single-leg prototype as early as 1995, but the parking-lot hack proved the idea was production-ready. The Lefty was fast-tracked to market.

2000 — Lefty Launches

The first production Lefty fork debuted to a skeptical world. A single-legged fork looked wrong — fragile, unbalanced, unfinished. But Brian Lopes won the dual slalom at Sea Otter that year on a Lefty-equipped bike, and the performance argument was made. The original Lefty offered 100mm of travel, weighed significantly less than comparable forks, and was dramatically stiffer. Riders either loved it or hated it, but nobody ignored it.

2000s — Evolution & Specialization

Throughout the 2000s, the Lefty family expanded. Carbon fiber versions appeared, saving significant weight. The Lefty Max pushed travel to 130–140mm for trail riding. The Lefty Speed line targeted XC racers with lighter damping and lockout controls. The Lefty 2.0 platform brought the needle bearing system into a more refined, maintainable package. Electronic lockout (ELO) systems appeared and disappeared. Each generation reduced friction, saved weight, and improved damping.

2018 — Lefty Ocho: The Revolution

The Lefty Ocho (Spanish for "eight") marked the eighth generation and the biggest change in the fork's history. For the first time, the Lefty became a single-crown design, ditching the iconic dual-crown setup that had defined it for 18 years. The Ocho used a standard tapered steerer tube (1.5" to 1-1/8"), making it compatible with any modern frame — no more proprietary headset requirements. Internally, the four-sided stanchion was replaced with a three-sided Delta Cage design, reducing friction and weight. A new Chamber Damper delivered class-leading small-bump sensitivity. The Ocho shed 250g from its predecessor.

2019+ — Oliver & Beyond

Cannondade extended the Lefty concept to gravel with the Lefty Oliver, a 30mm-travel single-sided fork designed specifically for the Topstone Carbon gravel bike. The Oliver uses the same needle bearing technology but tuned for the smaller impacts and longer rides of gravel. It represents the first time Lefty technology has been adapted beyond mountain biking, proving the concept works across disciplines. Today Lefty forks appear on XC hardtails, full-suspension trail bikes, gravel bikes, and even e-bikes.

Why "Lefty"?

The name comes from the obvious visual cue — the fork only has a left leg. But it also reflects Cannondale's willingness to go in a direction nobody else would. As the company puts it: "We had to go a little left field." The Lefty remains the only production single-sided suspension fork in cycling history, protected by an extensive patent portfolio that no competitor has been able to work around.
